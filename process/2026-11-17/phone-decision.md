# Fake phone on the screen, or the real phone? (to be sure of our decision)

Question: in Echo, does the phone appear **inside the game** (a fake phone on the computer screen), or is it **the player’s real phone**, connected like in the Netflix game *Unhinged*?

Current decision (2026-11-17): the real phone, each student with a computer and a phone, at their own pace, the same experience on iPhone and Android. This document checks that decision against references and against our own project.

## 1. A collection of references

### A fake phone on the screen (the “found phone” genre)
| Game | What the phone does | What we can learn |
|---|---|---|
| **Sara Is Missing** (Kaigan Games, 2016) | The whole game is a phone interface that looks almost like a real one; you read messages and look at photos. | Reading someone’s private messages creates intimacy and guilt, with no setup. |
| **Simulacra** (Kaigan Games, 2017) | A full simulated phone (browser, mail, social media, a dating app). The phone glitches and behaves strangely as you go deeper. | The phone itself can “break” to show a state: this echoes Echo’s bugs. |
| **A Normal Lost Phone** (Accidental Queens) / **Another Lost Phone** | The story is introduced through a fake smartphone interface. | Good for a calm story told through messages and files. |
| **Replica** (Somi, 2016) | A found-phone mystery. | The player becomes an investigator of someone else’s phone. |

Common pattern: the player **snoops** through messages, mails, photos and history to work out what happened to the owner. The phone belongs to someone else.

### The real phone as part of the game
| Game | What the phone does | What we can learn |
|---|---|---|
| **Unhinged** (Netflix, Night School Studios), the game we use as a reference | You scan a QR code on the TV or computer to link your phone. The phone is a controller: its tilt guides the flashlight on screen. When a call comes in the story, **your real phone rings** and the audio plays on its speaker. | The real phone makes the fiction reach into the room. The QR code is the moment the phone becomes part of the story. |
| **Netflix games on TV in general** | The phone or tablet is the controller; the controller may disconnect if the phone’s screen turns off (Netflix help centre). | Real risk to plan for: the connection depends on the phone staying on. |
| **Jackbox / AirConsole** (known party games, not verified in this research) | Each player uses their own phone as a controller on the same screen. | Phones work well for a group in the same room. To verify before we rely on them. |

In Unhinged the phone belongs to **the player**. In the found-phone games it belongs to **someone else**. That difference is the heart of our choice.

## 2. What changes for Echo

| | Fake phone on the screen | Real phone (our decision) |
|---|---|---|
| **Whose phone is it?** | A phone inside the story: the player watches Nexus. | The player’s own phone: the player lives Nexus. A message from the group arrives to *her*. |
| **Nomophobia** (a keyword of the pitch) | Shown, not felt: we see a phone that buzzes. | Felt: the player holds the object, looks down, looks up, can put it down or not. |
| **The cold message / being left out** | One more bubble on screen. | A sound, a shake, the screen going grey in her hand. The exclusion reaches her body. |
| **Attention** | One place to look. The story flows. | Two places to look (computer and phone). Risk of missing the story while reading messages; the research found second screens can interrupt flow. |
| **The journal** | A panel on screen, visible to neighbours. | Private on her phone: she can write without being watched. |
| **Setup in class** | None. Works offline. | A QR code, a server, Wi-Fi, phones that stay awake, sound allowed. More that can fail. |
| **iPhone / Android** | No difference. | Differences (no vibration on iPhone): we chose the same experience for all, without vibration. |
| **A student without a phone** | Not a problem. | Needs a fallback (a fake phone on screen). |
| **Storyboard** | One flow: scenes and messages in the same shot. | Two tracks to write: what happens on the screen and what happens on the phone, and **when the player must look down** (the screen darkens, as you described). |
| **Cost to build and test** | Low. | Higher: server, QR code, phone page, more things to test. |

## 3. What the research does and doesn’t tell us
- It found **no study** that directly compares a fake on-screen phone with a real phone for narrative immersion.
- Related findings: a second screen can be too great an interruption for the player’s flow (a paper on second-screen game companions); the timing between two screens matters for immersion (UCL); a natural mapping between a gesture and its effect improves immersion (CHI 2014), which is relevant if we ever bring back the pointer.
- So the choice rests on **our intention** (nomophobia, exclusion felt on the body) more than on proof. It needs a test with teenagers.

## 4. A way to be sure
- Keep **both**: the real phone as the main experience, and a **fake phone inside the game** for students without a phone, or when the Wi-Fi fails. This also lets us compare the two.
- Test **one scene** (0:12, the video and the cold message) in both versions with a few teenagers. Ask them what they felt when the message came, and where they looked.
- Decide with three questions: does the real phone make them feel the exclusion more? Do they lose the thread of the story? Does it work in a real classroom (Wi-Fi, phones, time)?
