## ID card: Echo
**Question:** *If I'm not the funny one, not the smart one, not the popular one… then who am I?*

**Tells:** You don't have to become someone else to belong.

- **Age:** 14
- **Time online:** ~7 hours a day, mostly scrolling and watching other people live their lives
- **Concept:** A teen in an identity crisis. He doesn't know who he is or where he fits in class, so he keeps trying on other people's roles without ever feeling at home in any of them.
- **Username:** Echo (he only reflects other people's voices and has none of his own yet)

**Skills**
1. **Mimicry:** copies the jokes, style and opinions of whoever he's with, so he blends in anywhere but belongs nowhere.
2. **Reading the room:** spends so much time observing others that he notices every social shift before anyone else.
3. **Shape-shifting online:** switches aesthetics, profile pics and personas depending on the trend, with several accounts and no real "him".

## Clichés and archetypes
Archetypes and familiar narrative codes can ease identification, understanding and immersion in a game or story.

### Why it works
1. **Familiarity:** recognizing a situation or character gives reference points to understand it faster.
2. **Schema theory:** the brain organizes knowledge into mental structures; familiar codes activate them and ease learning new information.
3. **Character identification:** recognizable emotions, motivations and experiences let players project themselves into the story.
4. **Cognitive load:** known conventions take less effort to interpret, leaving attention for the experience and new mechanics.
5. **Narrative transportation:** a familiar situation eases entry into a fictional world, as long as it stays coherent.

An archetype is a recognizable structure; a cliché is a representation made predictable or simplistic by repetition.

### Familiarity ≠ realism
A familiar element is not more realistic, just easier to recognize and accept. Example: the hero rejected by their community is a known archetype; even in an unrealistic world, the need to belong is understandable.

Design hypothesis (not an automatic chain, but consistent with research):
**Familiarity → understanding → possible identification → immersion**

Start from something the player knows, then gradually introduce the unexpected.

### Critique of a game character
Why is she starting to be mean to people and making chaos just because she feels apart? She could simply speak to people ("I don't understand"). She could have done so much with this spy headphone and only uses it to make chaos. It is frustrating, and when people hear her thoughts they hate her even more. We play a role that is sad at first and ends up hated by everyone — what is the purpose? I would have loved to have the choice to do whatever I want with this headphone.
